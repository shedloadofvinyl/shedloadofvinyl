What is Shedload of Vinyl?

Shedload of Vinyl is a women's advocacy site about emotional manipulation, power imbalances and the slow loss of trust in your own judgement.

It is for the grey areas. The things that do not always look serious from the outside, but change how you behave over time.

Maybe you are always the one explaining yourself. Maybe disagreement feels strangely risky. Maybe you find yourself checking whether you are being "fair" while the other person never seems to have to do the same.

The point of this site is simple: if a relationship repeatedly leaves you confused, guilty, frightened of conflict or unsure of your own memory, that is worth paying attention to.

Power dynamics

One person's feelings always matter more

If they are upset, everything stops. If you are upset, you are expected to calm down, be reasonable or get over it.

Over time, you can start treating their discomfort as urgent and your own as optional.

They get to define what is reasonable

Your boundaries are described as excessive. Their demands are described as normal.

Your privacy is suspicious. Their secrecy is none of your business.

The rules are not always spoken out loud, but somehow they keep favouring the same person.

You need permission without anyone calling it permission

No one says, "You are not allowed."

Instead, there may be sulking, guilt, coldness, arguments or a bad atmosphere until doing what you want stops feeling worth it.

Experience, age, money or confidence gets used as authority

Someone may repeatedly imply that they understand relationships better, know how the world works, or are simply more rational than you.

That can make ordinary disagreement feel like proof that you are naive, immature or unstable.

Gaslighting and reality distortion

You remember something clearly. They tell you it did not happen like that.

Not once, but often enough that you start checking yourself before you speak.

You begin saying things like, "Maybe I remembered it wrong."

Your interpretation is always the least credible one

You say something felt cruel. They say you are too sensitive.

You say something felt controlling. They say they were only trying to help.

You say you felt uncomfortable. They say you are imagining motives that are not there.

The goalposts move

You answer one criticism and another appears.

You explain yourself, then the problem becomes your tone. You change your tone, then the problem becomes your timing. You wait, then you are accused of avoiding the issue.

There is no version of the conversation where you quite get it right.

You start relying on other people to tell you what is normal

You ask friends to read messages. You explain arguments in detail. You want someone outside the relationship to tell you whether your reaction makes sense.

That can happen when your own judgement has been challenged too many times.

Common manipulation patterns

Selective affection

Warmth, attention and closeness can disappear when you disagree and return when you fall back into line.

You may start chasing the good version of the relationship instead of asking why affection feels conditional.

Guilt instead of a direct request

Instead of saying what they want, they make you feel selfish for not already wanting it too.

You end up agreeing because saying no feels cruel.

Double standards

Behaviour that is unacceptable from you is understandable from them.

If you need space, you are cold. If they withdraw, they are protecting their peace.

If you ask questions, you are suspicious. If they ask questions, they are being caring.

Blame reversal

You raise a problem and end up defending yourself.

The original issue gets replaced by your reaction, your character, your history or something you once did months ago.

Keeping you slightly off balance

Approval, affection and reassurance may feel inconsistent.

You can become more focused on reading their mood and getting things back to normal than on whether the relationship is actually fair.

Making independence feel like betrayal

Time alone, outside friendships, hobbies, work or private thoughts can be treated as distance from the relationship.

You may slowly give up pieces of your own life just to avoid the friction.

Reactive abuse

The phrase is often used for situations where someone is repeatedly provoked, cornered, criticised, lied to, baited or emotionally worn down until they react badly, and that reaction is then presented as if it happened in a vacuum.

You are pushed past your limit

You may shout, swear, send angry messages, slam a door or say something you regret.

Your reaction still matters. But so does the context that led up to it.

Your reaction becomes the only evidence anyone sees

A calm person can still be manipulative. A distressed person can still be reacting to something real.

Volume, tone and composure do not tell you who held the power in the wider relationship.

You get cast as the abusive one

The focus moves onto your worst moment.

You become so busy proving that you are not dangerous, unstable or controlling that the pattern you were reacting to disappears from view.

Responsibility still goes both ways

Being provoked does not make every reaction acceptable.

But one angry reaction is not automatically the same thing as a sustained pattern of manipulation, intimidation, blame or control.

What it can do to you

You second-guess ordinary decisions

You start thinking through how they might react before deciding what you want.

You lose confidence in your own memory

You remember something, then immediately wonder whether you are being unfair.

You become hyper-aware of mood

You can tell from a face, a pause or a text message that the atmosphere has changed.

You start adjusting yourself before anything has even been said.

You become more interested in keeping the peace than being honest

You stop raising certain topics because you already know where they go.

You do not need a perfect label. If a relationship repeatedly makes you feel smaller, confused, guilty, frightened of disagreement or unable to trust yourself, that is enough reason to take it seriously.

What you can do

You do not have to decide exactly what the relationship is before you do anything. If something feels wrong, you can start by giving yourself a bit more room to see it clearly.

Write down what happened

If it is safe, keep brief notes somewhere private. Date, what happened, what was said, and how the conversation ended.

Do not write an essay. The point is to stop every incident being rewritten in your head later.

Look for patterns, not one-off rows

One argument can be messy. What matters more is what keeps repeating.

Do you always end up apologising? Do they keep changing the subject? Do your boundaries keep becoming debates? Do you feel more confused after talking than before?

Reality-check with someone you trust

Choose someone who is steady and not involved in the relationship.

You do not need them to tell you what to do. Sometimes it is enough to say, "Can I tell you what happened and you tell me if this sounds normal to you?"

Give yourself private space

Keep hold of things that are yours. Your friendships. Your money where possible. Your own email. Your own passwords. Your own plans.

If someone has access to your devices or accounts, review what they can see from a device you trust.

Try small boundaries and watch what happens

You do not need a huge confrontation.

A small boundary can tell you a lot. Saying no. Taking time to reply. Seeing a friend. Keeping something private. Asking for a direct answer.

Pay attention to whether the boundary is respected or punished.

Stop trying to win every argument

If the conversation keeps going in circles, you do not have to keep proving your point.

You can end a discussion without getting agreement. You can say, "I remember it differently," or "I'm not discussing this any further."

Notice what happens to you afterwards

After an argument, do you feel clearer or more confused? Safer or more anxious? More confident or ashamed?

Your body and mood can sometimes tell you more than the final words of the argument.

Get specialist support before things feel extreme

You do not need to be in immediate danger to speak to a support service.

You can contact one simply because you are confused, worried, or want help making sense of a pattern.

You do not need permission to take your own discomfort seriously. You also do not have to make a big decision all at once. Sometimes the first step is just getting enough distance and support to think clearly.
